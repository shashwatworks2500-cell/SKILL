# Conversion-Oriented UX

Load when structuring a page around a business goal.

**These are decision frameworks, not templates.** Never assemble a page by pattern-matching an industry. Read the brief, identify the actual decision the user is making, then apply the relevant thinking.

## Establish the goal first

Before structuring anything, answer:

1. **The one outcome** the client is paying for.
2. **The primary action** that achieves it — a single, nameable thing.
3. **Secondary actions** for users not ready to take the primary one.
4. **The friction** — what makes someone hesitate here? Price uncertainty, trust, complexity, commitment, comparison difficulty?
5. **Journey stage** — is this audience discovering, comparing, or deciding?

If 1 and 2 are unanswered, ask. Everything downstream inherits them.

## Universal principles

**The primary action must be obvious without reading.** Squint until text is illegible — the eye should still land on it first.

**Match commitment to journey stage.** "Request a valuation" is right for a decided user and wrong for a first-time visitor who needs "See recent sales". Offer both, weighted correctly.

**Reduce friction before adding persuasion.** Removing three form fields usually outperforms adding a testimonial section.

**Trust is built with specificity, not claims.** "Trading since 1974, four staff, one workshop in Bristol" outperforms "industry-leading quality". Concrete detail is unfakeable; superlatives are free and therefore worthless.

**Never manufacture proof or urgency.** No invented testimonials, statistics, logos, review counts, countdowns, or "3 spots left". If the client has not supplied it, build the slot empty or omit the section. Fabricated proof is the fastest way to destroy the trust the page exists to create.

**Give a path for the unready.** Most visitors will not convert today. A secondary path — a guide, a phone number, a saved search — captures value the primary CTA loses.

## Lead generation

*Decision: "is this the right firm to contact, and is contacting them low-risk?"*

- **CTA hierarchy:** one primary contact action, repeated at decision points. One lower-commitment secondary ("See our work", "Download the guide").
- **Friction reduction:** the shortest form that enables a reply. Name, contact, message. Every additional field costs submissions — justify each one or remove it.
- **Trust:** real credentials, named team members, genuine case detail, verifiable accreditations. Specific work examples beat any badge.
- **Multiple contact paths:** form, phone, email, WhatsApp where appropriate. Some users will never use a form — a phone number displayed as text (and `tel:` linked on mobile) converts a distinct audience.
- **Response expectation:** state it. "We reply within one working day" measurably reduces submission anxiety.
- **After submit:** confirm specifically, say what happens next and when. Never a bare "Thanks".

## E-commerce

*Decision: "is this the right product, and can I buy it without risk?"*

- **Discovery:** the path from landing to product must be short. Search, category navigation, and filtering are the primary interface — treat them as such, not as chrome.
- **Category navigation:** reflect how customers think, not internal taxonomy. Filters need visible applied state and a clear reset.
- **Product pages:** honest imagery at real scale, complete specifications, stock status, delivery cost and timing *before* checkout. Unexpected delivery cost at checkout is the most common abandonment cause.
- **Comparison:** make it possible without losing place — persistent specs, comparison view, or open-in-new.
- **Cart visibility:** always-visible item count; confirm additions without a full page change; make editing quantity and removal easy.
- **Checkout:** guest checkout available · progress visible · one column · address autocomplete · inline validation · payment methods shown early · nothing that navigates away mid-flow.
- **Risk reduction:** returns policy, warranty, and delivery terms stated where the decision happens, not only in the footer.

## Real estate

*Decision: "is this property worth my time to visit?"*

- **Discovery:** search and filter by the criteria buyers actually use — location, price, bedrooms, type. Persist filters across navigation; losing a search is losing the user.
- **Location:** the dominant factor. Map, area context, transport, schools, amenities. An address alone is insufficient.
- **Pricing:** visible and unambiguous on listing and detail. "Price on application" loses serious buyers unless the market genuinely requires it.
- **Visual storytelling:** photography is the product. Large, high-quality, ordered as a walkthrough. Floor plans, video, and virtual tours where available. Image quality directly determines enquiry rate.
- **Amenities:** scannable and structured, not a prose paragraph.
- **Enquiry flow:** book a viewing, ask a question, request details — distinct actions with different commitment levels. Attach the property reference automatically so the user never types it.
- **Trust:** named agents with real contact details, genuine credentials, honest listing history.
- **Saved properties** and comparison — buyers evaluate over weeks, not minutes.

## Luxury and premium brands

*Decision: "does this brand deserve my money and my identification?"*

- **Restraint:** the primary signal. Fewer elements, more space, no urgency, no discount language. Pressure destroys premium positioning instantly.
- **Typography:** carries the brand. Considered pairing, wide measure, generous leading, significant display/body contrast.
- **Imagery:** editorial quality, full-bleed, uncropped by decoration. A single exceptional image beats a grid of adequate ones. Never stock.
- **Whitespace:** compositional, not leftover.
- **Storytelling:** provenance, craft, materials, people, process. The narrative *is* the value proposition.
- **Interaction quality:** precise, responsive, unhurried. Sloppy interaction reads as cheap faster than any visual flaw.
- **Conversion is quiet:** a single well-placed action. No repeated banners, no popups, no countdowns.
- **Density low**, journey slow. The user is being persuaded to identify with something, not to act quickly.

## Local businesses

*Decision: "are they open, can they do this, and how do I reach them now?"*

Often mobile, often urgent, often comparing two or three options in minutes.

- **Contact above everything:** phone number visible immediately, `tel:` linked, tappable. Most conversions here are calls, not forms.
- **Location:** address, map, parking, directions. "Serving Bristol and surrounding areas" needs a specific base.
- **Opening information:** current hours, holiday variations, and whether they are open *right now* — the actual question being asked.
- **Services:** plainly listed in customer language, not trade jargon. Include what they do *not* do; it saves everyone time.
- **Trust:** real reviews (never invented), genuine accreditations, years in business, named people, photographs of actual work.
- **Pricing:** indicative ranges or "from" pricing where possible. Total opacity loses price-sensitive local customers to a competitor who published a number.
- **Mobile-first, genuinely:** this audience is on a phone, possibly outdoors, possibly in a hurry. Large targets, minimal typing, no multi-step forms for a simple enquiry.

## Anti-patterns

Never implement these, regardless of who requests them:

- Fabricated testimonials, statistics, logos, ratings, or user counts
- False urgency or scarcity — countdowns, "3 left", fake stock pressure
- Entry popups that block content before any value is delivered
- Exit-intent interstitials on mobile
- Forms demanding information irrelevant to the next step
- Hidden costs revealed only at checkout
- Fake "live" activity notifications
- Confirmshaming ("No thanks, I don't want to save money")
- Newsletter gates in front of ordinary content
- Contact pages with no phone number when phone is the audience's preferred channel

These raise short-term metrics and cost the client trust, reputation, and repeat business. If a client requests one, say plainly why it will cost them and offer the honest alternative that achieves the same goal.
